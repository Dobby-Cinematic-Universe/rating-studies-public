\section{First study: twelve questions}\label{sec:first}

\subsection{Design}\label{sec:firstdesign}

\paragraph{Excerpts.} A pool of 5{,}000 candidate excerpts was drawn at random, 2{,}500 from videos and 2{,}500
from livestreams, so that the first passage of a recording is a random passage of a random recording. Both
models rated every candidate at 250 words. 4{,}902 candidates are eligible; the others were unreadable for
raters (mostly text in another script) or called unrateable by a model. The study used 160 excerpts, one per
recording, 80 from videos and 80 from streams:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{56 at random}, with the three periods 2015--2018, 2019--2021 and 2022--2025 in proportion. They are a
  random sample of the population and give its figures directly, with wide intervals.
\item \textbf{104 chosen by rule on the two models' scores} and on nothing else. A random sample holds few
  excerpts that tell much: most passages score low on most questions, and the two models seldom differ. The rule
  (a greedy D-optimal design over the twelve questions) adds, one at a time, the excerpt that most sharpens a
  regression of people's ratings on what both models say and on where they differ.
\end{itemize}
All 160 excerpts were read before any participant saw them. Three were replaced by the same rules: two held
text in another script and one concerned a third person who could be identified.

\paragraph{Questions.} The highest-loading question of each of the eight dimensions and the four
highest-loading of the rest, among the questions on which the two models' recording averages correlate at 0.6
or more. They alternate between two forms, so that nobody answers more than six per excerpt
(Appendix~\ref{app:wording}).

\paragraph{Raters and sets.} Each form splits the 160 excerpts into 20 sets of eight. A participant reads one
set, and each set was completed by five participants who passed two checks: an instructed-response item, and a
reading check on the time before the first answer. Participants who failed were paid like the others and their
place was given again. 205 participants are valid, five per set and six in five sets
(Section~\ref{sec:materials}).

\subsection{Estimators}\label{sec:firstmethods}

The reliability of one rating is estimated by a one-way analysis of variance with the excerpts as groups, and
that of the mean of five follows from equation~\eqref{eq:rel}. Because 104 excerpts were chosen by the models'
scores, a raw correlation among the 160 would be too high. What that choice leaves untouched is the regression
of the mean rating $H_i$ on the two models' scores,
\begin{equation}
  H_i = a + \bm b^\T \bx_i + e_i + \bar\varepsilon_i , \qquad \bx_i = (A_i, B_i)^\T .
\end{equation}
With $\bSigma$ the covariance of the two scores in the population (taken from the eligible pool, every recording
once and the two kinds equally), the figures for the population are rebuilt as
\begin{equation}
  \hat\sigma_T^2 = \hat{\bm b}^\T\bSigma\hat{\bm b} + \widehat{\var}(e), \qquad
  \R{5} = \frac{\hat\sigma_T^2}{\hat\sigma_T^2 + \hat\sigma_\varepsilon^2/5}, \qquad
  r_m = \frac{(\bSigma\hat{\bm b})_m}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{mm}\,\hat\sigma_T^2}}\sqrt{\R{5}} ,
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\var}(e)$ is the residual variance of the regression less the raters' noise in $H$. This assumes
a straight-line relation and the same noise everywhere. The verdict is taken on these figures, with the verdict
of the 56 random excerpts alone beside it; where the two differ, the question is reported as \verd{open}.
Intervals come from resampling excerpts 1{,}000 times within part and kind. On simulated studies with a known
answer the rebuilt figures were unbiased, and the raw correlation among all excerpts was too high, as expected.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Four questions are usable, firmly.} For positive tone, disappointment, self-critical wording and
  self-disclosure, the whole interval of the reliability lies above 0.60 and that of each model's correlation
  above 0.50, on both sets of figures (Figure~\ref{fig:verdicts}, Table~\ref{tab:verdicts}).
\item \textbf{Eight questions turn on the raters.} Four are borderline on both sets of figures (energetic, humor,
  compassion, hedging) and four are open (calm, persistence, narrative, persuasion). The models meet their line
  on all eight; what is missing is agreement among five raters.
\item \textbf{No question is misread, and none has an unclear rubric.} The lowest reliabilities, 0.45 for hedging
  and persuasion, are not far above the line of 0.40.
\item \textbf{The two models are level}: over the twelve questions 0.673 for \jev{} and 0.670 for \drex{}, a
  difference of $+0.003$ ($-0.012$ to $+0.018$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A change during collection.} The reading check was changed while the main batch ran. As fixed in
advance, an excerpt counted as not read when fewer than 15 seconds passed before the rater asked for the
questions; raters who asked at once and read with the first question beside the excerpt were excluded although
they spent one to two minutes on a page. The check was changed to the time before the first answer, on times and
check results alone, without looking at ratings. Under the check as first fixed, 178 of the 205 participants
remain and the verdicts are the same with one exception: persuasion falls just under the line for an unclear
rubric. The four usable questions stay usable. (This comparison needs page timings, which are not part of the
public data.)

\subsection{Added after the results had been read}\label{sec:firstadded}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A model against an unlimited panel.} Dividing out the raters' noise, each model correlates with
  the panel consensus $T$ at 0.86 on average over the twelve questions (0.83 to 0.90 and 0.83 to 0.89), and a
  single rater at 0.51. On every question both models are closer to the consensus than one rater is.
\item \textbf{Dimensions from two markers.} Four dimensions had two rated questions. Taken together, the two
  markers give a composite reliability of 0.65 (F1), 0.88 (F2), 0.84 (F3) and 0.70 (F6), with the models'
  dimension scores at 0.71 to 0.83, so all four pass the rule (Table~\ref{tab:s2repdim}). For F1 and F6 this
  was found after the fact, and it is what the second study set out to confirm.
\item \textbf{Known groups.} Which questions are rated higher in streams than in videos is nearly the same for
  people and for the models: across the twelve questions the differences correlate at 0.92 with either model
  among the 56 random excerpts.
\item \textbf{Levels.} \jev{} rates hedging 1.2 scale points higher than people do, and \drex{} rates nine of the
  twelve questions lower than people.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What the first study left open.} Four dimensions rested on one question each. The five raters of a
set read the same eight excerpts, so the general level of a set's raters could not be told from its excerpts.
The figures had to be rebuilt from a regression. Every rater saw sample answers, which might pull them towards
the models. And each model had rated each excerpt once.
